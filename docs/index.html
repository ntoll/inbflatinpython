Welcome to the concert hall

InB♭InPython is a virtual concert hall with two creative spaces.

The rehearsal room is the light one you arrived from: a code editor containing a musical score as Python code, and a Play button.

The stage is the dark one: press Play and twenty musicians - each a short video, everyone playing in the key of B♭ - wait in the wings for your code to call on them. The top of the stage contains the Stop button, which returns you to the code, and log messages that tell you what's going on.

The code is the score, and you are the conductor.

This documentation tells the story of the score, from one solo musician to a full interactive production. Along the way, small examples run live right here in the page (press the Run buttons - they are real Python, in your browser, courtesy of importpython.org, built upon Browser Friendly Python and MicroPython). Each idea ends with a link that opens the real rehearsal room with an illustrative example score, ready to read, modify and perform.

Musical terms are explained as they arrive; no musical training is assumed, only curiosity and a willingness to share playful creative coding.

Your first musician

A performance is a special Python function - one declared with async def, which simply means "this code knowingly unfolds through time". Inside it, await means "wait here until this is done" (you're awaiting something to finish). That's the only programming theory you really need to know.

To get a feel what await does before any music is involved, run this - and notice the awaits (where we call asyncio.sleep() for a couple of seconds) are real pauses in the execution: 

import asyncio

print("a beat...")
await asyncio.sleep(2)
print("another beat...")
await asyncio.sleep(2)
print("rests are part of the music.")

Here is the smallest score that makes a sound: 

from inbflat import enter, start


async def performance():
    """One musician, one entrance."""
    await enter("mallets")


start(performance())

enter() brings a musician on stage - their video fades up in a randomly chosen seat and begins to play - and start() is the downbeat that sets the whole performance going. When the musician is finished, they fade away to a ghost image of themselves: a visible memory of their contribution.

Rests

In music, a rest is written silence - as deliberate as any note. On this stage, await rest(6) is six seconds of patience; await rest(2, 10) randomly chooses somewhere between two and ten seconds of silence. Rests are where the listening happens, and they are what turn a list of entrances into a piece: 

from inbflat import enter, rest, start


async def performance():
    """Three entrances, with room to listen between."""
    await enter("rhodes keyboard")
    await rest(6)
    await enter("clarinet")
    await rest(2, 10)
    await enter("vocals")


start(performance())

Rolling the dice

Terry Riley's "In C" (1964) and Darren Solomon's "In Bb 2.0" (2009) - the two ancestors of this piece - are aleatoric music: composed chance (from the Latin alea for dice). The composer decides what is possible; the dice decide what happens at the moment of performance. In Python the dice are the random module, and random.seed() is the beautiful trick that makes chance repeatable: the same seed deals the same random shuffle, every time.

Here's band.py - that defines the list of musicians.

And the deal - run it twice; it never changes because we set a seed (1935):

import random
from band import MUSICIANS


def shuffle(items):
    """Fisher-Yates shuffling."""
    for i in range(len(items) - 1, 0, -1):
        j = random.randrange(i + 1)
        items[i], items[j] = items[j], items[i]


random.seed(1935)
names = list(MUSICIANS)
shuffle(names)
print("tonight's first five, for seed 1935:")
for name in names[:5]:
    print("  " + name)

That is MicroPython's own shuffle, so it deals exactly as the stage does. On the stage, all of this is one line: arrange() shuffles the musicians and returns your orchestra. Change the seed and press Play again: a different group of musicians will appear. Keep the seed the same and press Play again: the same group will appear in exactly the same order. 

import random
from inbflat import arrange, enter, rest, start

random.seed(42)  # Change this number to change the seed.
orchestra = arrange(musicians=6)


async def performance():
    """The dice decide who plays, and in what order."""
    for musician in orchestra:
        await enter(musician)
        await rest(4, 9)


start(performance())

The dice are fair, which also makes them heartless: the musician you love may simply not be dealt. arrange(featuring=...) names one performer - or a list of them - each guaranteed a place in the orchestra; the dice still choose everyone else, and still choose where in the order your featured musicians appear. 

import random
from inbflat import arrange, enter, rest, start

random.seed(2009)  # The year of "In Bb 2.0".
orchestra = arrange(musicians=6, featuring="plastic toy sax")


async def performance():
    """Six are dealt; the toy sax is promised a seat."""
    for musician in orchestra:
        await enter(musician)
        await rest(6)


start(performance())

Polyphony

Polyphony is a musical term we use to mean "how many voices sound at once". Twenty all at once is a glorious cacophony; three to five is a subtle texture; one soloist is a uniquely personal encounter through sound.

arrange(musicians=8, polyphony=2) caps the stage at two performers at once: everyone else waits in the wings. Each rest(), after the stage is full, holds on until a musician exits, then counts its silence before the next musician enters - so the pause always sits between an exit and an entry. 

import random
from inbflat import arrange, enter, rest, start

random.seed(43)
orchestra = arrange(musicians=8, polyphony=2)


async def performance():
    """Two voices at once; the rest wait in the wings."""
    for musician in orchestra:
        await enter(musician)
        await rest(2, 5)


start(performance())

Hear two voices take turns - it opens in the rehearsal room with this score in the editor, ready for Play.

The cap set when arranging is not a law of nature. Calling polyphony() mid-performance changes it on the spot: thin the texture towards a solo, or open it wide. Nobody is evicted when the cap drops - the stage simply admits no one new until enough voices have finished: 

import random
from inbflat import arrange, enter, polyphony, rest, start

random.seed(1964)  # The year of "In C".
orchestra = arrange(musicians=8, polyphony=4)


async def performance():
    """An intro of four voices, a solo, an outro of three."""
    for musician in orchestra[:4]:
        await enter(musician)
        await rest(3)
    await rest(8)
    polyphony(1)  # Entries now wait for an empty stage.
    await enter(orchestra[4])  # The solo, once truly alone.
    await rest(10)
    polyphony(3)  # The outro opens the texture again.
    for musician in orchestra[5:]:
        await enter(musician)
        await rest(3, 8)


start(performance())

Thin the texture mid-piece - it opens in the rehearsal room with this score in the editor, ready for Play.

Playing together

Chance is only one way to compose. You can also through-compose: write every entrance yourself, no random musical dice at all.

For that you will want simultaneity - ensemble() performs several things as one and returns when the last has finished. And the musicians comes pre-sorted into sections in the inbflat module, as orchestras do: strings, winds, keyboards, electronics, percussion and voices are ordinary Python lists you can loop over - or ignore, and compose your own by mood. 

from inbflat import enter, ensemble, rest, start


async def performance():
    """The winds arrive as one, then a string joins them."""
    await ensemble(
        enter("harmon trumpet"),
        enter("clarinet"),
        enter("plastic toy sax"),
    )
    await rest(10)
    await enter("acoustic violin")


start(performance())

Hear the winds arrive as one - it opens in the rehearsal room with this score in the editor, ready for Play.

Dynamics

Dynamics are music's louds and softs. A crescendo grows; a diminuendo quietens; and al niente - "to nothing" - is the musical term for a fade that ends in silence. When a musician's part says tacet, they simply stop playing and sit quietly; on this stage tacet() fades them al niente (sound and video together), and frees their voice for whoever waits in the wings. They can always enter() again later.

The crescendo() and diminuendo() functions work in the same way: they take a musician's name, a target volume (between 0.0 [silence] and 1.0 [loudest]), and a duration in seconds over which the change occurs.

The tacet() function takes a musician's name and an optional duration (niente) in seconds, over which to fade to silence. If the niente argument is omitted, the fade occurs over 4 seconds, if it is set to 0, the musician suddenly stops.

It's also possible to set the musician's initial dynamic level when they enter the stage, by passing a dynamic argument to enter(). The value should be between 0.0 (silence) and 1.0 (loudest). 

from inbflat import (crescendo, diminuendo, enter, rest,
                     start, tacet)
 
 
async def performance():
    """Louder, softer, and away to nothing."""
    await enter("mallets", dynamic=0.3)
    await rest(10)
    await crescendo("mallets", to=1.0, over=4)
    await rest(6)
    await diminuendo("mallets", to=0.3, over=4)
    await rest(6)
    await tacet("mallets", niente=8)
 
 
start(performance())

Hear the mallets rise, sink and vanish - it opens in the rehearsal room with this score in the editor, ready for Play.

The lighting desk

A followspot is the moving beam that picks a soloist out of the dark; a wash is even light across the whole stage, where nobody is special. followspot("electric guitar") brightens one musician and dims the rest; calling it on someone else moves the light; wash() restores equality. Purely visual - pair it with a crescendo when you want ears and eyes agreeing about who matters. 

from inbflat import (enter, ensemble, followspot, rest,
                     start, wash)


async def performance():
    """Three arrive; the light finds one; then an even wash."""
    await ensemble(
        enter("electric guitar"),
        enter("electric bass"),
        enter("kaoss pad"),
    )
    await rest(8)
    followspot("electric guitar")
    await rest(8)
    wash()


start(performance())

See the followspot move - it opens in the rehearsal room with this score in the editor, ready for Play.

Words on the stage

One of the twenty performers speaks rather than plays: "Information", a is beautifully thoughtful poem written and read by Daniel Donahoo, whose lines rise across the stage as the poem is recited.

You can also make use of this capability for your own words.

caption() floats one line of your own; proclaim() gives words the big, centred, still treatment of the poem's close; both take an optional hold argument in seconds (with defaults of 6 and 12 respectively). The recite() function takes a whole timed sequence of (seconds, text) pairs - your poem, should you have one. 

from inbflat import caption, enter, proclaim, rest, start


async def performance():
    """A musician, a caption, and a proclamation."""
    await enter("acoustic piano")
    await rest(6)
    await caption("words can join the music")
    await rest(8)
    await proclaim("and some arrive with weight")


start(performance())

See words join the music - it opens in the rehearsal room with this score in the editor, ready for Play.

The quickest way to make this piece entirely your own is to write some words. recite() takes a whole poem as (seconds, text) pairs - the seconds counted from the moment of the call - and sets its lines rising across the stage while the music plays on. Here is a small orchestra beneath a poem about why any of us write code at all, its final two lines given proclaim()'s big still treatment: 

import random
from inbflat import arrange, enter, proclaim, recite, rest, start

random.seed(8080)
orchestra = arrange(musicians=3)


async def performance():
    """A small texture beneath your own words."""
    for musician in orchestra:
        await enter(musician)
        await rest(3)
    recite([
        (0, "Code runs our world."),
        (5, "Yet code isn't written for computers."),
        (11, "We write code because we are human beings"),
        (14, "imbued with ideas, imagination and feelings."),
        (19, "Code expresses our thoughts made digital:"),
        (22, "a medium for our values, cultures and ways of life."),
        (28, "If you knew code, what would you create?"),
        (35, "For those who venture into the world of code"),
        (38, "will be the ones creating our futures."),
    ])
    await rest(44)
    await proclaim("What will your contribution be..?")
    await proclaim("The only way to find out, is to try.")


start(performance())

The audience's instrument

"In C" and "In Bb" are both examples of community music-making. This site encourages community music-making by sharing code. But the keys() function enlarges that community to the audience: a row of soft round keys along the foot of the stage, each sounding a note of the B♭ major pentatonic scale - five notes chosen so that nothing you press can be wrong. A timbre is a sound's character - the reason a trumpet and a violin playing the same note sound different. The keys come with four pre-defined timbres: "bell", "pluck", "breath" and "drone". 

from inbflat import caption, enter, keys, rest, start
 
 
async def performance():
    """A drone settles, then the keys are yours."""
    await enter("ebow")
    await rest(6)
    keys("pluck")  # Or "bell", "breath" and "drone"
    await caption("press a key - whatever you play belongs")
 
 
start(performance())

Settle a drone and play along - it opens in the rehearsal room with this score in the editor, ready for Play.

The keys answer the computer keyboard too: the digits 1 to 9 strike the first nine keys, 0 the tenth, and each key lights up as it sounds - the log says so when the row appears: keys ready, click to play or type 0-9. And where a pointer can only press one key at a time, a keyboard can press several digits at once: the audience can play chords.

The available notes are a musical decision too, and flats and sharps are both welcome. Here the audience gets the B♭ blues scale - the pentatonic's streetwise cousin, whose flattened fifth makes everything sound knowing - so whatever they press is up to something: 

from inbflat import caption, enter, keys, rest, start
 
 
async def performance():
    """A blues palette for the audience, spelled in sharps."""
    await enter("electric bass")
    await rest(6)
    keys("pluck", notes=["Bb3", "C#4", "D#4", "E4", "F4", "G#4"])
    await caption("a B flat blues - press something bent")
 
 
start(performance())

The key's timbre is nothing more than a function that makes a sound.

Here is a bell built from first principles - an oscillator and an envelope - with a button to ring it. Change the frequency, the wave, the ramps: this is how instruments are born, and a function like ring, taking the audio context and a frequency, is exactly what keys(timbre=...) accepts. MDN explains the raw materials beautifully: the oscillator that makes the tone, and a full tutorial on shaping sounds with envelopes.

And here's the Python code to make the bell ring:

from bfp import web, window

audio = None


def ring(event):
    """One soft bell, built from first principles."""
    global audio
    if audio is None:
        audio = window.AudioContext.new()
    now = audio.currentTime
    tone = audio.createOscillator()
    tone.type = "sine"
    tone.frequency.value = 233.08  # B flat.
    level = audio.createGain()
    level.gain.setValueAtTime(0, now)
    level.gain.linearRampToValueAtTime(0.2, now + 0.02)
    level.gain.exponentialRampToValueAtTime(0.001, now + 2)
    tone.connect(level)
    level.connect(audio.destination)
    tone.start(now)
    tone.stop(now + 2.1)


web.page["ring"].on_click = ring

Seats and props

The stage has twenty seats in a grid, five across and four deep, numbered 0 to 19 reading left to right, top to bottom. (Musicians count from one and programmers from zero; the management is aware of the dispute.) The dice normally choose seating, but enter(name, seat=...) places a musician exactly, and prop() seats any element you can build with bfp.web - a sign, a picture, a button of your own devising. Props take seats but not voices: the polyphony ignores furniture. 

from bfp import web
from inbflat import enter, prop, rest, start


async def performance():
    """A front row arranged by hand, and one prop."""
    await enter("mallets", seat=0)
    await rest(3)
    await enter("acoustic guitar", seat=2)
    await rest(3)
    await enter("acoustic violin", seat=4)
    await rest(3)
    prop(web.p("(reserved)"), seat=3)


start(performance())

Make your music

Every link above works because of a small honest trick: the Share button in the rehearsal room puts your whole score into the address after the # - the URL fragment, which never leaves your browser - and copies the link. Send it anywhere; whoever opens it finds your score in their editor. There is no server, no account, no saving: a performance simply is its link.

So: change a seed. Compose a section of your own. Fade the whole band al niente and proclaim something. Write a poem and recite() it over the drones. Build an instrument nobody has heard. The twenty musicians of "In Bb 2.0" have been waiting many years in the wings for a conductor exactly like you - and whatever you do, it will be in B♭, so it cannot be wrong.

Have fun! Discover your voice! Share your music!
